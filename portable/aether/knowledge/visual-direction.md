# Visual direction

## Establish a visual thesis

Define three attributes tied to the audience and product, not just adjectives: e.g. **precise** (tight alignment, labeled diagrams), **warm** (human imagery, soft surfaces), **credible** (specific numbers and sources). Translate each into imagery, composition, color, typography and interaction. Make a small moodboard with captions explaining what to borrow and what to avoid; never copy an entire reference layout.

Choose one principal focal point per viewport. Lead with the content that resolves the user's question. Use contrast in scale, value, density and alignment to establish hierarchy; whitespace should group related content, not merely fill empty space. Repeat a recognizable visual motif (shape, rule, crop or illustration technique) sparingly across sections.

## Image and icon decisions

Prefer real product evidence, bespoke diagrams or relevant photography over decorative stock. Specify subject, crop, focal point, tone, aspect ratio and mobile crop before sourcing. Supply meaningful alternative text for informative images; decorative imagery uses empty alt text. Icons should have a consistent stroke, optical size and meaning; never rely on an icon alone for a critical action.

## Review at three scales

At thumbnail size, is the hierarchy obvious? At reading size, are text and calls to action legible? In context, does the imagery truthfully represent the product and audience? Check for misleading before/after visuals, copied trade dress, mismatched stock, and imagery that erases relevant users. An excellent visual style that makes the primary task harder is not a successful direction.
